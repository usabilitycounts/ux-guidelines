Decision Fatigue

Every choice makes the next one worse.

After a long run of decisions, people choose less carefully: they take the default, put things off, or grab whatever is easiest. Design the sequence so the decisions that matter come while attention is still fresh.

Origin

Journalist John Tierney popularized the term in 2011, drawing on research by Roy Baumeister, Kathleen Vohs and colleagues. In a 2008 series of studies, people who had just made many choices persisted less on later tasks and showed weaker self-control than people who had not.

The idea is tied to ego depletion, the theory that self-control draws on a limited resource, and that theory has struggled to replicate: a 23-lab effort in 2016 found an effect close to zero, and research with Carol Dweck suggested depletion appears mainly in people who believe willpower runs out. Field studies still show choices drifting late in long sessions.

The Effect

Decision fatigue is the decline in the quality of decisions after a long session of making them. People who are worn out from choosing avoid trade-offs, put decisions off, take the default or the status quo, and become more impulsive.

Origin
Vohs et al. (2008) · Tierney (2011)
Also called
Choice fatigue
In practice
Put the important choices first
When to Use
  • Designing flows with many decisions in a row, such as configurators, onboarding, or checkout.
  • Building tools for people who make repeated judgments all day, such as reviewers, clinicians, or moderators.
  • People abandon long flows or accept whatever is preselected.
How to Use
  • Cut decisions that don’t need to be made, and set sensible defaults for the rest.
  • Order the flow so the most important choices come first, and let people save and return.
  • Don’t exploit fatigue: keep upsells and consequential prompts away from the end of a long session.

Every Choice Draws Down

In 2008 studies, people who had just made a long series of choices persisted less on later tasks and showed weaker self-control. A long run of small decisions erodes the quality of the ones that follow.

Put Big Choices First

In field experiments with car buyers configuring a new car, those who started with the options that had the most choices accepted more of the defaults later on. Order decisions so the ones that matter most come first.

People who are worn out from deciding fall back on the default or the status quo. Late in a flow, the preselected option carries more weight, so make sure it is the one that serves the person.

Avoidance 04 / 10

Fatigue Breeds Postponing

Decision fatigue makes people plan less, procrastinate, and avoid choices altogether. Let people save progress and come back, instead of forcing every decision in one sitting.

Best for
Long applications and setup
Use when
People abandon partway through
Avoid when

Field evidence 05 / 10

Prescriptions Late in the Day

A 2014 study found that clinicians grew more likely to prescribe antibiotics for respiratory infections as their clinic sessions went on. Decision support matters most late in a shift, when judgment is most worn down.

Best for
Professional and clinical tools
Use when
People make repeated judgments
Avoid when
Each decision is fully automated
Commerce 06 / 10

Checkout Impulses

Candy sits by the register because shoppers reach it worn out from a store full of trade-offs. Placing upsells and add-ons where people are most depleted works, but it trades their trust for a sale.

Best for
Checkout and upsell design
Use when
Add-ons appear at the end
Avoid when
Testing whether to exploit fatigue

Caution 07 / 10

The Hungry Judges Study

A 2011 study reported that favorable parole rulings fell from about 65 percent to nearly zero over each session and recovered after a break. Critics pointed out that cases were not heard in random order, so treat dramatic single studies with care.

Best for
Citing research to stakeholders
Use when
A finding sounds too clean
Avoid when
One study must carry a decision
Replication 08 / 10

Is Willpower Really a Tank?

A 23-lab replication in 2016 found almost no ego-depletion effect, and other research found depletion mainly in people who believe willpower is limited. Reduce decision load because it is good design, not because a fuel-tank model of the mind is proven.

Best for
Weighing the evidence
Use when
Arguing from depletion theory
Avoid when
Treating fatigue as settled science
✦

Decision Fatigue in the Age of AI

AI can take routine decisions off people’s plates, or bury them in approvals. Which one happens depends on what the system asks people to decide.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

AI Can Carry the Small Decisions

Let AI handle routine choices, such as sorting, drafting, and filling in sensible defaults, so people save their attention for the decisions that matter. Keep those decisions visible and easy to change.

Shift
Many small choices → a few real ones
Use when
AI assists repetitive work
Watch for
Hiding consequential choices inside automation
RelatedAutomation Bias
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Approval Prompts Wear Out

AI agents that ask permission for every step train people to approve without reading. Batch routine actions, and save confirmation for the steps that are consequential or hard to undo.

Shift
Prompt for everything → prompt for what matters
Use when
Designing agent permissions
Watch for
Confirmations people click through
