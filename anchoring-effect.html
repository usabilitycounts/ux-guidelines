Anchoring Effect

The first number sets the frame.

People judge a value by starting from whatever number they saw first and adjusting too little. Prices, presets, baselines, and estimates all carry an anchor, whether you choose it or not.

Origin

Anchoring was first noticed in psychophysics: when people judge a series of stimuli, they compare the rest against the first and last. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman formalized it in their 1974 paper on judgment under uncertainty as anchoring and adjustment: people start from an initial value and adjust, but not far enough.

Their demonstrations were striking. Given five seconds to estimate 8 × 7 × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1, people guessed a median of 2,250; given the same numbers in ascending order, the median was 512. The answer is 40,320. Later, Dan Ariely and colleagues found that people with higher last two digits of their social security number bid 60 to 120 percent more for the same items.

The Effect

When people estimate a value, they start from whatever number is at hand, the anchor, and adjust away from it too little. The anchor works even when it is obviously irrelevant, and knowing about the effect barely weakens it.

Origin
Tversky and Kahneman (1974)
Also called
Anchoring bias, anchoring and adjustment
In practice
Choose the first number people see
When to Use
  • Showing prices, plans, discounts, or any number people will judge.
  • Setting preset values in forms, sliders, tips, or donations.
  • Asking people, or a team, for estimates.
How to Use
  • Decide which number people see first, because it frames everything after it.
  • Use only real reference values: genuine earlier prices and clearly labeled baselines.
  • When you need an unbiased judgment, ask for it before showing any number.
The classic 01 / 10

The Wheel of Fortune

Before estimating the share of African countries in the United Nations, people watched a wheel stop on 10 or 65. Those who saw 10 guessed 25 percent on average; those who saw 65 guessed 45 percent. A number they knew was random moved their answer.

Best for
Explaining the effect
Use when
Estimates follow an arbitrary number
Avoid when
People already know the real value

Pricing 02 / 10

The First Tier Sets the Scale

A high-priced option sets the reference that makes the others look reasonable, the way a car looks affordable next to a more expensive model. Order plans and tiers deliberately, and make sure every tier is one people would really choose.

Best for
Pricing pages and plan tables
Use when
Several tiers are offered
Avoid when
A tier exists only as a decoy

Discounts 03 / 10

Was and Now

A struck-through original price anchors the sense of a bargain. Show only prices that were really charged: EU rules now require a stated earlier price to be the lowest one charged in the 30 days before the discount.

Best for
Sales and discounts
Use when
A real earlier price exists
Avoid when
The original price was never charged

Inputs 04 / 10

Presets Pull Answers Toward Them

Preset donation amounts, tip percentages, and slider starting points anchor what people enter. Choose presets on purpose, and leave fields blank when you need people’s own estimate.

Best for
Donation, tip, and quantity inputs
Use when
Presets help people decide quickly
Avoid when
You need an unbiased estimate

Research 05 / 10

Questions Can Plant Numbers

Asking whether something takes more or less than 50 hours anchors the estimate that follows. In surveys and interviews, ask for open estimates before offering any reference value.

Best for
Surveys and interviews
Use when
Asking for estimates
Avoid when
Comparison to a benchmark is the point
Dashboards 06 / 10

Baselines Are Anchors

The comparison you show, whether last month, a target, or an industry average, decides whether a number reads as good or bad. Choose baselines deliberately and label them clearly.

Best for
Metrics and reporting
Use when
Showing performance over time
Avoid when
The baseline would mislead

Teams 07 / 10

The First Number in the Room

In group discussions, whoever speaks first can have a disproportionate effect on the outcome. Collect estimates independently before discussing them, as planning poker does.

Best for
Estimation, planning, and critiques
Use when
Teams estimate together
Avoid when
Data will settle the answer anyway
Remedy 08 / 10

Consider the Opposite

Warnings barely help: people told that an anchor would contaminate their answer, or paid to be accurate, were still pulled by it. The most reliable remedy is to list reasons the anchor might be wrong.

Best for
Decision reviews
Use when
A number came before the judgment
Avoid when
No anchor was present
✦

Anchoring in the Age of AI

An AI’s first suggestion becomes the anchor people edit from. Where independent judgment matters, it should come before the machine’s.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

The First Suggestion Anchors

An AI’s first draft, estimate, or recommendation becomes the starting point people adjust from instead of rethinking. For estimates and judgments, ask people for their own answer before showing the AI’s.

Shift
Blank page → AI anchor
Use when
AI suggests numbers or drafts
Watch for
Showing the AI’s estimate before people think
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Numbers in the Prompt

Studies have found that language models show anchoring too: a number in the prompt can pull an estimate toward it. Leave figures out of prompts when you want the model to judge independently.

Shift
Neutral prompt → seeded prompt
Use when
Asking AI for estimates or ratings
Watch for
Prompts that hint at the answer
