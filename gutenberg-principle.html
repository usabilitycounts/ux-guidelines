Gutenberg Principle

From top-left to bottom-right.

On evenly weighted pages the eye sweeps from top-left to bottom-right. The Gutenberg principle maps that reading gravity — and where to put what matters.

Origin

Edmund C. Arnold was an American newspaper designer and journalism professor, often called the father of modern newspaper design. Over a career spanning Syracuse and Virginia Commonwealth universities he redesigned hundreds of papers and wrote more than two dozen books on publication layout.

The diagram is named for Johannes Gutenberg only by association with print; it was Arnold who described reading gravity — the habitual top-left to bottom-right sweep trained by Western text — and turned it into a practical layout tool.

The Principle

On evenly weighted pages, the eye sweeps from the primary optical area at top-left to the terminal area at bottom-right, giving the two fallow corners far less attention. Put openings top-left and calls to action bottom-right.

Coined by
Edmund C. Arnold
Source
Newspaper layout practice and teaching
In practice
CTA bottom-right, brand top-left
When to Use
  • You are laying out a plain-text terms page or a newsletter where nothing but type fills the screen.
  • A localization team asks where the logo and sign-up button should sit in the Arabic and Hebrew builds.
  • Pricing has been parked in the bottom-left of a dense document page and support keeps hearing that customers never saw it.
How to Use
  • Place the primary call to action in the terminal area at bottom-right, where the diagonal sweep naturally comes to rest.
  • Move legal boilerplate and footnotes into the weak fallow corner, and never hide anything a reader must act on there.
  • Check whether the page is actually homogeneous before applying the four quadrants, because one strong image replaces gravity with an F-pattern.

The bottom-left quadrant gets the least attention of all four. The eye sweeps past it on the way down and to the right — for many users, content placed here may as well not exist.

Quadrant 4 05 / 10

Terminal Area

The bottom-right quadrant is where the eye rests after sweeping across the page. It’s the natural place for a call to action — the eye literally ends up here, ready for the next step.

Gravity 06 / 10

Reading Gravity

The diagonal pull from Primary to Terminal. Reading gravity is the default — what happens when no other visual hierarchy interrupts the sweep. Reinforce it with content that supports the journey.

Axis 07 / 10

Axis of Orientation

The eye sweeps left to right along horizontal axes of orientation as it drops down the page with reading gravity. Moving against that flow costs effort — which is why content placed in the fallow corners gets so little attention.

Effect

Applies 08 / 10

When It Applies

The Gutenberg Principle describes a default reading pattern — what happens with evenly distributed, visually homogeneous content. Newspaper-style layouts, prose-heavy pages, dense documents.

Homogeneous content
Cultural

Override 09 / 10

When It Doesn’t

Any strong visual element overrides reading gravity. F-pattern or Z-pattern emerge in their place. Most modern web pages — with images, buttons, and varied type — break Gutenberg entirely.

Replaced by

Practical Applications

Where the diagram still applies: editorial layouts, newsletters, dense article pages, terms of service, and any composition that needs to feel formal and traditional. Put the byline top-left, the CTA bottom-right, and let gravity do the rest.

Gutenberg in the Age of AI

A conversation has no quadrants — when the answer is returned directly, the four-zone reading model gives way to a single vertical stream.

✦ AI Era

Chat Has No Quadrants

A conversation is a single vertical column with no Primary or Terminal zones. Reading gravity flattens into a simple top-to-bottom stream of turns.

Shift
Quadrants → stream
Use when
Linear conversation UI
Watch for
Losing the CTA position

The Answer Replaces the Layout

When AI returns the answer directly, there’s often no page to lay out at all. The four-quadrant diagram gives way to a single, generated response block.
