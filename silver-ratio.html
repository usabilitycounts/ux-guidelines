Silver Ratio

One to two point four one four.

The silver ratio, 1 + √2 or about 2.414, divides space more decisively than the golden ratio. It turns up in octagons and A4 paper, and it gives layouts a long, calm proportion with one clearly dominant part.

Origin

The silver ratio has been studied since antiquity under other names, because of its ties to the square root of 2, the regular octagon, and the Pell numbers, whose ratios 5/2, 12/5, 29/12, and 70/29 close in on it. In a regular octagon, the width across the flats is exactly 1 + √2 times the length of a side.

Its name is recent, coined by analogy with the golden ratio: where φ solves x² = x + 1, σ solves x² = 2x + 1. ISO 216 paper, including A4, uses the closely related 1 : √2, which keeps its shape when folded in half. Cut the largest square from an A4 sheet and the strip left over is a silver rectangle.

The Ratio

Two lengths are in the silver ratio when the larger is to the smaller as twice the larger plus the smaller is to the larger. The result, σ ≈ 2.414, gives a longer, more emphatic division than the golden ratio: about 71/29 instead of 62/38.

a / b = (2a + b) / a = σ = 1 + √2 ≈ 2.414

Origin
Studied since antiquity · named after the golden ratio
Also called
Silver mean
In practice
Octagons, A4 paper, 71/29 splits
When to Use
  • Dividing a wide layout, banner, or panel into one dominant area and one narrow one.
  • Choosing proportions for hero images, cards, or crops that should feel long and calm.
  • Working with A-series paper or octagonal shapes, where the ratio is already built in.
How to Use
  • Split a length at about 71/29, or make a rectangle about 2.4 times as wide as it is tall.
  • Remove two squares from a silver rectangle and the remainder is another silver rectangle, which gives a repeatable grid.
  • Round to a practical ratio such as 12:5 or 29:12, and trust your eye over the decimal.
The geometry 01 / 10

Two Squares and a Remainder

Cut two squares from a silver rectangle and what is left is a smaller silver rectangle. Repeat and you get a nested grid, the silver counterpart of the golden rectangle’s single square.

Best for
Building a proportional grid
Use when
A layout needs nested divisions
Avoid when
A simple even grid is enough

Layout 02 / 10

A 71/29 Split

Dividing a page at the silver ratio gives a dominant main area and a narrow sidebar, a more decisive split than the golden ratio’s 62/38. It suits content beside navigation, filters, or a table of contents.

Best for
Main content with a sidebar
Use when
One area should clearly dominate
Avoid when
Both areas matter equally

Banners 03 / 10

Wide Heroes and Banners

A rectangle about 2.4 times as wide as it is tall sits close to the 2.39:1 frame of widescreen cinema, so it reads as wide and cinematic. It leaves room for a headline beside the subject rather than on top of it.

Best for
Hero images and banners
Use when
A wide, calm frame is needed
Avoid when
The subject needs height, as in portraits

Paper 04 / 10

Hidden in A4

A-series paper uses 1 : √2, so halving a sheet keeps its shape. Cut the largest square from an A4 page and the leftover strip is a silver rectangle, so print layouts inherit the ratio for free.

Best for
Print and PDF layouts
Use when
Designing for A-series paper
Avoid when
The output is screen only

Shape 05 / 10

The Octagon

A regular octagon is 1 + √2 times as wide across its flats as each side is long. Octagonal badges, icons, and emblems carry the ratio inside them, which is one reason they look balanced.

Best for
Icons, badges, and emblems
Use when
Drawing eight-sided shapes
Avoid when
The shape has nothing to do with octagons
Numbers 06 / 10

Pell Ratios Stand In

The Pell numbers 1, 2, 5, 12, 29, 70 grow by roughly 2.414 at each step, so ratios such as 5:2, 12:5, and 29:12 make practical stand-ins. A 1200 × 500 banner is 12:5, within half a percent of σ.

Best for
Picking pixel dimensions
Use when
Exact irrational sizes are impractical
Avoid when
Templates demand exact sizes
Type 07 / 10

A √2 Type Scale

The silver ratio’s close relative √2, about 1.414, makes a steep type scale such as 12, 17, 24, 34, and 48. Each level is clearly distinct from the next, which suits pages with few, strong levels of hierarchy.

Best for
Display-heavy pages
Use when
Hierarchy needs strong contrast
Avoid when
Dense interfaces need many close sizes
Caution 08 / 10

Which Silver Ratio?

In Japan, the term silver ratio (白銀比) often means 1 : √2, the Yamato ratio used in character design and architecture, not 1 : 2.414. When briefing a team, give the number rather than just the name.

Best for
Briefs and specifications
Use when
Working with international teams
Avoid when
Everyone shares one definition

The Silver Ratio in the Age of AI

Generative tools default to a few familiar frames and repeat a lot of folklore about proportion. Ask for the ratio you want and check what you are told.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

Proportion on Request

Image and layout generators default to familiar frames such as 16:9 and 1:1. Name the ratio you want, such as 12:5, so the output follows your system instead of the tool’s default.

Shift
Default frame → specified ratio
Use when
Generating layouts or images
Watch for
Accepting whatever frame the tool picks
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Folklore in the Answers

Ask AI about famous proportions and it may repeat claims that buildings, logos, or faces follow one ratio or another. Measure before you cite, since many of those claims don’t hold up.

Folklore → measurement
Use when
Researching proportion with AI
Watch for
Citing claims without sources
