Choice Architecture

There is no neutral way to present a choice.

How options are presented, from which one is preselected to the order they appear in, shapes what people choose. Some design is unavoidable, so design it on purpose and in people’s interest.

Origin

Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein coined the term in their 2008 book Nudge. Anyone who arranges options, from a cafeteria manager to a form designer, is a choice architect, they argued, and can steer people toward better choices without forbidding any option. They called the philosophy libertarian paternalism.

With John Balz they listed the tools: defaults, expecting error, understanding mappings, giving feedback, structuring complex choices, and creating incentives. Other researchers split the elements into those that structure the set of choices, such as the number of options and the default, and those that describe them, such as how attributes are grouped and labeled.

The Framework

Choice architecture is the design of how options are presented: how many there are, how they are ordered and described, and which one is selected by default. Because no presentation is neutral, every interface that offers a choice is already steering it. The question is only in which direction.

Origin
Thaler and Sunstein (2008)
Also called
Decision environment design
In practice
Defaults, order, and framing
When to Use
  • Designing forms, settings, plans, or any screen where people choose between options.
  • Setting defaults for privacy, notifications, subscriptions, or plan tiers.
  • A choice has long-term consequences people tend to put off, such as saving or security.
How to Use
  • Choose defaults that serve most users, and make changing them easy.
  • Limit or group options, and add filters or comparisons when many are needed.
  • Describe attributes in terms people care about, and show the consequences of each choice.
Defaults 01 / 10

Defaults Decide

Most people keep whatever is preselected. In one study, organ donor registration was twice as high where people had to opt out as where they had to opt in. Pick the default that serves the user, not just the business.

Best for
Settings, plans, and sign-up forms
Use when
One option suits most people
Avoid when
No option suits most people

Active choice 02 / 10

Ask Instead of Assuming

When no default suits most people, require an active choice: nothing is preselected and people must pick before continuing. It costs a moment, but it avoids silently giving many people the wrong thing.

Best for
Consequential personal preferences
Use when
Preferences vary widely
Avoid when
The choice slows people down for nothing
Overload 03 / 10

Fewer Options or Better Tools

Each extra option takes time to evaluate, and too many can stall a decision. Either limit the alternatives or add decision aids such as filters, sorting, comparisons, and recommendations.

Best for
Catalogs, plans, and pickers
Use when
Options run into dozens
Avoid when
Experts want the complete list
RelatedHick’s Law
Order 04 / 10

Order Is a Nudge

Search results, rankings, and recommendations shape what people pick, because the first and most prominent options get chosen more. Rank by what serves the user, not by what earns the most.

Best for
Search results, lists, and menus
Use when
Ranking or recommending options
Avoid when
Users set the sort order themselves
RelatedSerial Position Effect
Framing 05 / 10

Translate the Attributes

Convert numbers into terms people care about: gallons per 100 miles instead of miles per gallon, the total cost instead of the monthly payment. Evaluative labels such as good or poor make numbers easier to compare.

Best for
Pricing and comparison tables
Use when
Numbers are hard to compare
Avoid when
The audience already works in the metric
Grouping 06 / 10

Partitions Shape Allocation

People tend to spread resources evenly across whatever categories are offered, such as the funds in a retirement plan. The number and grouping of options changes the outcome, so group with intent.

Best for
Budgets, portfolios, and allocations
Use when
People split resources across options
Avoid when
Categories are fixed by the domain
Time 07 / 10

Commit Now, Act Later

People favor the present over the future. Thaler and Benartzi’s Save More Tomorrow plan had employees commit in advance to save part of future raises, which raised savings without asking for sacrifice today.

Best for
Savings, health, and habit features
Use when
Benefits arrive in the future
Avoid when
The action must happen now
Ethics 08 / 10

For the User, Not Against

The same tools that help people can be turned against them. When choice architecture serves the business at the user’s expense, it becomes a dark pattern. Ask whether the design points where users would go if they had time to think.

Best for
Design reviews and audits
Use when
Business and user goals diverge
Avoid when
Never: there is no neutral option

✦

Choice Architecture in the Age of AI

AI now sets the defaults and ranks the options: suggested replies, autofill, recommended plans, the single answer. That is choice architecture at scale.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

Defaults Set by Models

Suggested replies, smart autofill, and predicted settings are defaults chosen by a model for each person. Make them easy to change, and show the alternatives rather than hiding them.

Shift
Fixed defaults → predicted defaults
Use when
AI preselects options
Watch for
Hiding the alternatives
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

One Answer, No Choice

An assistant that returns a single recommendation removes the choice set entirely. For consequential decisions, show a few options with their tradeoffs instead of one pick.

Shift
Ten results → one answer
Use when
AI recommends products or plans
Watch for
A single pick for a high-stakes choice
