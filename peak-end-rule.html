Peak-End Rule

Experiences are judged by their peak and their end.

Daniel Kahneman’s research shows people judge an experience by its most intense moment and its final one — not its average. Design the peak, stick the landing, and fix the worst moment first.

Origin

Daniel Kahneman, the Nobel-winning psychologist, established the rule with Barbara Fredrickson in the early 1990s — most famously in a 1993 cold-water study where a longer trial with a slightly warmer ending was preferred over a shorter, objectively better one.

The work separated the experiencing self from the remembering self: what we choose to repeat is decided by the memory, not the experience. Colonoscopy studies with Donald Redelmeier confirmed it clinically — endings changed how whole procedures were remembered.

The Rule

People judge an experience largely by how it felt at its peak — the most intense point, good or bad — and at its end, rather than by the sum or average of every moment. Duration barely registers; the remembered shape is what returns.

Kahneman and Fredrickson (1993)

In practice
Design peaks and endings
When to Use
  • A checkout ends on a bare success page, and nobody has decided what the last screen should actually leave behind.
  • Retention is flat despite a shorter flow, because the redesign shaved seconds without giving the experience any memorable high point.
  • Cancellation is handled by a legal-looking confirmation dialog that becomes the final thing a departing customer remembers about you.
How to Use
  • Map the journey and mark its single worst moment, then fix that crash, surprise charge, or lost form before polishing anything.
  • Engineer one deliberate high point per flow, placed where value actually lands, such as the first successful publish or import.
  • Rewrite every final screen to confirm what happened, summarize the result, and offer one obvious next step instead of a dead end.

The Peak-End Rule in the Age of AI

A conversation with a model is an experience with a peak and an end. The last answer — and the worst one — is what the user carries away.

In the Age of AI 07 / 08

The Last Answer Wins

Users judge a whole session by the final response. A conversation that ends on a correction, a refusal, or a half-answer is remembered as a failure — close with the resolved answer and a clean next step.

The final response

In the Age of AI 08 / 08

Recovery Is the Peak

When the model gets something wrong, the correction moment becomes the emotional peak of the session. A fast, gracious recovery is remembered better than a session that was merely fine throughout.
