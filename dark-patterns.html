Dark Patterns

Win the click, lose the trust.

Interfaces crafted to trick people into buying, subscribing, or sharing more than they meant to. They lift a metric for a quarter, then cost the trust of the people who were tricked, and increasingly they draw fines.

Origin

Harry Brignull, a UK user experience designer, coined the term on 28 July 2010 when he registered darkpatterns.org, a pattern library meant to name and shame deceptive interfaces. The site has since moved to deceptive.design, and in 2023 he published the book Deceptive Patterns.

Research and regulators followed. A 2019 study by Arunesh Mathur and colleagues at Princeton crawled about 11,000 shopping sites and found 1,818 instances of dark patterns. In 2022 France’s CNIL fined Google and Facebook over cookie banners, and in 2025 Amazon settled the FTC’s case over Prime sign-up and cancellation for $2.5 billion, without admitting wrongdoing.

The Pattern

A dark pattern is an interface crafted to trick people into doing something they didn’t mean to do, such as buying, subscribing, or sharing data. It works by exploiting habits and biases, and it trades short-term conversion for long-term trust and, more and more often, legal risk.

Origin
Harry Brignull (2010)
Also called
Deceptive design patterns
In practice
Would users choose this if they understood it?
When to Use
  • Designing sign-up, checkout, cancellation, or consent flows.
  • A metric such as conversion or retention is being pushed hard.
  • Reviewing a flow for honesty before launch, or after a complaint.
How to Use
  • Give the choice the business wants and the choice the user may want the same visual weight.
  • Show the full price up front, and let people leave the way they joined.
  • Ask of each flow whether users would make the same choice if they fully understood it.
Subscriptions 01 / 10

The Roach Motel

Easy to get in, hard to get out. The FTC said Amazon’s Prime cancellation flow, known internally as Iliad, routed people through repeated pages of offers. Under EU pressure in 2022, Amazon made cancellation two clicks in Europe.

Best for
Subscriptions and memberships
Use when
Designing how people leave
Avoid when
Canceling takes more steps than joining

Pricing 02 / 10

Drip Pricing

A low headline price, with fees and charges added step by step until checkout. By the time the real total appears, people have invested enough effort that many pay it anyway. Show the full price from the first screen.

Best for
Checkout and pricing
Use when
Fees or taxes apply
Avoid when
Fees appear only at the last step
Copy 03 / 10

Confirmshaming

A decline link worded to shame: No thanks, I don’t like saving money. It pressures people with guilt instead of persuading them with value. Word the decline option as plainly as the accept option.

Best for
Pop-ups, upsells, and newsletters
Use when
Offering something optional
Avoid when
The decline option mocks the user
Visual 04 / 10

Misdirection

A bright button for what the business wants and a faint link for what the user wants, or a consent box with a double negative. Software installers made it famous by bundling unrelated programs behind a prominent Accept button.

Best for
Consent and choice screens
Use when
Presenting yes and no
Avoid when
One option is hidden in plain sight
RelatedVon Restorff Effect
Privacy 05 / 10

Privacy Zuckering

Named after Mark Zuckerberg: designs that get people to share more than they intended. In 2022 France’s CNIL fined Google €150 million and Facebook €60 million because accepting cookies took one click and refusing took several.

Best for
Consent and privacy settings
Use when
Asking for data or tracking
Avoid when
Refusing takes more clicks than accepting
Urgency 06 / 10

Fake Urgency

Countdown timers that reset, and Running out! labels on stock that isn’t running out. In 2024 the UK’s Competition and Markets Authority secured undertakings from Wowcher over exactly these claims. Show only real deadlines and real stock.

Best for
Sales, offers, and booking
Use when
An offer really is limited
Avoid when
The deadline or scarcity is invented

Advertising 07 / 10

Bait and Switch

Advertise something free or cheap that most people can’t actually get, then steer them to a paid option. In 2022 Intuit agreed to a $141 million settlement over TurboTax ads for free filing that most filers didn’t qualify for.

Best for
Plans, tiers, and free offers
Use when
Promoting a free option
Avoid when
Most people cannot use the advertised offer
Remedy 08 / 10

Design for Informed Choice

The test for any flow: would people make the same choice if they fully understood it? Give yes and no equal weight, show the real total, and make leaving as easy as joining. Several jurisdictions now require exactly that.

Best for
Design reviews and audits
Use when
Before launch or after complaints
Avoid when
Never: honesty has no exceptions

✦

Dark Patterns in the Age of AI

AI can tailor copy, timing, and offers to each person. That makes persuasion more effective and manipulation cheaper and harder to see.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

Persuasion at Scale

A model can generate thousands of variants of a message and learn which ones work on whom. Hold generated copy to the same rules as handwritten copy, and never optimize for conversion alone.

Shift
One pattern → millions of variants
Use when
AI writes marketing or retention copy
Watch for
Optimizing only for conversion
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Opting Out of Training

In 2024 Meta planned to train AI on Facebook and Instagram posts, with an opt-out reached through misleading emails, login redirects, and a form that asked for a reason. Refusing consent for AI training should be as easy as giving it.

Shift
Buried opt-out → clear choice
Use when
Using customer data for AI
Watch for
Hiding the opt-out or demanding reasons
