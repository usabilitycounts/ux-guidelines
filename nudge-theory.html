Nudge Theory

Make the better choice the easier one.

A nudge changes the setting of a decision so the easy, automatic choice is the better one, while every option stays open. It works on habit rather than argument, which makes it effective, cheap, and easy to abuse.

Origin

Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein made the idea famous in their 2008 book Nudge, though the cybernetician James Wilk had used the term earlier. A nudge, they wrote, changes behavior predictably without forbidding any option or significantly changing incentives, and it has to be easy and cheap to avoid. Putting fruit at eye level counts; banning junk food doesn’t.

Governments took it up quickly. The UK founded its Behavioural Insights Team, the original nudge unit, in 2010, and similar units followed in Australia, Canada, Germany, Japan, the World Bank, and the European Commission. The evidence is contested: a 2021 meta-analysis reported real effects, a 2022 reanalysis found no evidence for nudging after correcting for publication bias, and nudge units’ own trials showed effects far smaller than published studies.

The Theory

A nudge alters the context of a choice so that fast, habitual thinking lands on the better option, without banning alternatives or changing incentives much. Defaults, placement, reminders, and showing what others do are the common levers.

Origin
Thaler and Sunstein (2008)
Also called
Nudging, behavioral insights
In practice
Easy and cheap to avoid
When to Use
  • Encouraging something people already intend to do, such as saving, finishing setup, or securing an account.
  • People act on habit or autopilot at the moment of choice.
  • Mandates or incentives would be too heavy-handed or too costly.
How to Use
  • Use the main levers: defaults, salience, social proof, timely reminders, and less friction.
  • Keep every nudge easy and cheap to avoid, and open about what it is doing.
  • Test nudges with your own users, since published effects often overstate real ones.
The classic 01 / 10

The Fly in the Urinal

Amsterdam’s Schiphol Airport etched an image of a fly into urinals to improve aim. It is the most cited nudge: a small change to the environment that works on automatic behavior without a single instruction.

Best for
Explaining the theory
Use when
The behavior is automatic
Avoid when
People need information to decide

Salience 02 / 10

Put the Better Option Within Reach

In snack shops at Dutch train stations, moving fruit and healthy snacks next to the register increased their sales. In an interface, the equivalent is placing the recommended action where attention already is.

Best for
Placement of actions and offers
Use when
One option serves users better
Avoid when
All options are equally good
Social proof 03 / 10

Show What Others Do

People look to others when unsure. Telling people how many neighbors pay on time, or which plan most customers choose, can move behavior, as long as it’s true and the norm is one worth following.

Best for
Onboarding, reminders, and plan choices
Use when
The desired behavior is common
Avoid when
The norm is low, and showing it backfires
RelatedSocial Proof
Friction 04 / 10

Make It Easy

The Behavioural Insights Team sums up its approach as EAST: make it Easy, Attractive, Social, and Timely. Removing one step from a flow often does more than any amount of persuasion.

Best for
Flows and forms
Use when
A behavior is intended but not done
Avoid when
Friction protects users from mistakes
Timing 05 / 10

Nudge at the Right Moment

A reminder works when it arrives at a moment people can act on it. Suggest a backup when the device is charging, not in the middle of a task.

Best for
Notifications and reminders
Use when
The behavior has a natural moment
Avoid when
The reminder would interrupt
Evidence 06 / 10

Smaller Than Advertised

A study of trials run by two nudge units found effects about a sixth the size of those in academic journals, and a 2022 reanalysis found no overall evidence after correcting for publication bias. Defaults tend to hold up; many other nudges are weak.

Best for
Planning experiments
Use when
Relying on published results
Avoid when
Effects were tested with your users
RelatedSurvivorship Bias
Personalization 07 / 10

One Nudge Doesn’t Fit All

Responses to the same nudge differ widely between people. Research suggests nudges tailored to individual differences work better than a single message for everyone, so segment and test.

Best for
Products with diverse users
Use when
Responses vary across groups
Avoid when
Personal data use would feel invasive
Ethics 08 / 10

Nudge, Not Sludge

Thaler calls friction that makes good choices harder sludge, like a cancellation maze. A nudge must be transparent and easy to avoid; when it serves the business against the user, it is a dark pattern.

Best for
Design reviews and audits
Use when
Business goals pull against users
Avoid when
Never: transparency is required

✦

Nudge Theory in the Age of AI

AI can tailor a nudge to each person’s habits, and it needs nudges of its own to keep people checking its work.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

Personalized Nudges at Scale

AI can tailor prompts and offers to each person’s habits and moods, which may make nudges more effective and harder to notice. Keep them transparent, and give people a way to see why they were shown something.

Shift
One nudge → one per person
Use when
AI personalizes prompts or offers
Watch for
Nudges people cannot see or refuse
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Nudging Toward Verification

Small prompts can counter overreliance on AI: a check-the-sources link, a confirmation before risky actions, highlighting the uncertain parts. Use nudges to slow people down where the AI is likely to be wrong.

Shift
Accept → review
Use when
AI output needs human checks
Watch for
Friction on every single answer
