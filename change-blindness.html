Change Blindness

If attention is elsewhere, the change never happened.

People miss surprisingly large changes when they happen during a blink, a reload, a cut, or a distraction. Interfaces that change in place without showing what changed leave users looking at a new screen while believing it is the old one.

Origin

In 1890 William James observed that we take in far less of a scene than we feel we do. Film editors learned it in practice: continuity errors between shots, such as a glass that refills or a scarf that changes color, routinely slip past audiences. In the 1970s George McConkie showed that words changed during a reader’s eye movement often went unnoticed.

The term took hold in 1997. Ronald Rensink, Kevin O’Regan, and James Clark showed that large changes to a photograph went unseen when a brief blank separated the two versions, and Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin named and reviewed the effect. In a 1998 field study, Simons and Levin swapped a stranger asking for directions for a different person mid-conversation, and about half the people did not notice.

The Effect

People notice a change only when their attention is on the thing that changed at the moment it changes. If the change happens during a blink, a reload, a cut, or a distraction, even a large difference can go unseen, while the viewer feels sure they would have caught it.

Origin
Rensink, O’Regan and Clark (1997)
Source
Psychological Science, 1997
In practice
Show what changed, where the user is looking
When to Use
  • Content updates after a save, a filter, or a background refresh.
  • A screen changes while the user is looking somewhere else, such as during a reload or behind a modal.
  • People monitor dashboards, queues, or feeds for changes in status.
How to Use
  • Update in place instead of reloading, and keep the user’s scroll position and focus.
  • Mark changed items with a brief highlight or animation near where the user is looking.
  • For important changes, add a persistent cue or a log, since a passing highlight can still be missed.
The classic 01 / 10

Hidden by a Flicker

In the flicker task, a photo alternates with a slightly altered copy, separated by a brief blank. Even large changes can take many cycles to find, sometimes more than a minute. Changes to the parts people care about are found faster.

Best for
Explaining the effect
Use when
A blank separates two states
Avoid when
The change is animated in place

Page loads 02 / 10

Reloads Erase the Difference

A full reload blanks the screen, which is the same disruption as the flicker. People who apply a filter or save a form can miss what changed. Update in place and keep the scroll position.

Best for
Filters, saves, and sorting
Use when
An action reloads the whole page
Avoid when
The whole page is meant to change

Feedback 03 / 10

Highlight What Changed

A brief highlight on an updated row, field, or total draws attention to where the change happened. Let it fade, so the screen settles back to normal.

Best for
Saved edits and live updates
Use when
Content changes after an action
Avoid when
Changes are constant and highlights become noise

Layout 04 / 10

Out of Sight, Out of Mind

Changes far from where the user is looking, such as a badge in the header or a total at the top of a long form, often go unseen. Put feedback next to the control that caused it.

Best for
Placing feedback and status
Use when
Feedback appears far from the action
Avoid when
The user is guided to the change

Noise 05 / 10

Distractions Mask Change

In the mudsplash studies, small blobs that appeared at the moment of change, away from it, were enough to hide it without any blank. Simultaneous animations, ads, and pop-ups do the same.

Best for
Busy, animated screens
Use when
Several things move at once
Avoid when
The change is the only motion
Dashboards 06 / 10

Silent Status Changes

A tile that turns red among dozens of others is easy to miss when nobody was watching it at that moment. Use alerts, persistent change markers, and a log of what changed.

Best for
Monitoring and operations screens
Use when
Values update while unattended
Avoid when
Someone watches every value

Choice 07 / 10

Choice Blindness

Johansson and Hall asked people to pick the more attractive of two faces, then secretly handed them the other one. Only about a quarter noticed, and many explained a choice they had not made. A clear confirmation step catches the mismatch.

Best for
Review and confirmation steps
Use when
Options look similar
Avoid when
Choices are trivial to undo
RelatedParadox of Choice
Concept 08 / 10

Confident and Wrong

People overestimate how well they detect changes, a finding called change blindness blindness. Designers make the same mistake about their own screens, so test whether users noticed instead of asking whether they would.

Best for
Planning usability tests
Use when
A team assumes a change is obvious
Avoid when
The change was tested with users
✦

Change Blindness in the Age of AI

AI rewrites, regenerates, and updates content in place. The more it changes at once, the easier it is to miss the one change that matters.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

Edits That Slip Past

When AI rewrites a document or a block of code, a changed number or a deleted clause looks just like the rest. Show a diff, mark the changed spans, and let people accept changes one by one.

Shift
Rewrite → tracked changes
Use when
AI edits existing content
Watch for
Silently replacing the whole text
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Answers That Update in Place

A regenerated or refined answer that replaces the previous one hides what is different. Keep earlier versions, and highlight what changed between them.

Shift
Regenerate → compare
Use when
Answers are regenerated or refined
Watch for
The old answer vanishes without a trace
