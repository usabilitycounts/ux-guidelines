Default Effect

Most people keep what they’re given.

Whatever happens when people do nothing is the option most of them end up with. Choosing a default is choosing for the majority, so it deserves as much care as the feature itself.

Origin

In 1988 the economists William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser documented status quo bias: people disproportionately stick with the current state of affairs. In 2001 Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea found that when a company enrolled new employees in its retirement plan automatically, participation rose sharply, and many stayed at the default contribution rate and fund.

In 2003 Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared organ donation across Europe: countries where people were donors unless they opted out had far higher consent than countries where people had to opt in. Defaults became the most reliable tool of nudge theory, and the one that best survives scrutiny.

The Effect

The default effect is the tendency to accept whatever option is preselected. It works through effort, since doing nothing is easiest; loss aversion, since the default becomes the reference point; and implied endorsement, since people read a default as a recommendation.

Origin
Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988) · Johnson and Goldstein (2003)
Also called
Status quo bias
In practice
Preselect what serves most users
When to Use
  • Setting initial values for settings, forms, plans, and privacy options.
  • Most people will never revisit the setting.
  • A choice has consequences people rarely think about, such as data sharing or renewals.
How to Use
  • Preselect the option that serves most users, not the one that only benefits the business.
  • Make defaults easy to see and easy to change.
  • Where preferences vary widely, use personalized defaults or ask for an active choice.
The classic 01 / 10

Opt Out or Opt In

Countries where citizens are organ donors unless they opt out have far higher consent than countries where people must opt in. The choice is identical; only the default differs.

Best for
Explaining the effect
Use when
A choice is framed as opting in or out
Avoid when
Everyone makes the choice actively

Enrollment 02 / 10

Automatic Enrollment

When new employees were enrolled in a retirement plan unless they declined, participation jumped, and many kept the default contribution rate and fund for years. A good default does the work of a thousand reminders.

Best for
Benefits, programs, and enrollment
Use when
Participation clearly helps the user
Avoid when
Enrollment commits people to unexpected costs
Why 03 / 10

Three Reasons Defaults Stick

Changing a default takes effort. The default becomes the reference point, so switching feels like a loss. And people assume whoever set the default chose it for a reason, which reads as a recommendation.

Best for
Understanding user behavior
Use when
A redesign changes what is preselected
Avoid when
Users actively compare every option
Settings 04 / 10

Most Settings Never Change

The defaults you ship are the experience most people get. Many never discover that alternatives exist, like the hidden choice of ringtones on a phone. Treat every default as a design decision, not an afterthought.

Best for
Settings and preferences screens
Use when
Shipping a new setting
Avoid when
Power users customize everything

Types 05 / 10

Mass, Smart, and Persistent

Mass defaults are the same for everyone. Smart defaults use what is known, such as location for currency. Persistent defaults remember the last choice, and adaptive ones update as people act. Pick the type that matches how much preferences vary.

Best for
Forms and checkout
Use when
Preferences vary by context
Avoid when
Using personal data would feel intrusive
Privacy 06 / 10

Privacy by Default

Defaults for data sharing decide what most people share. The EU’s GDPR, in Article 25, requires data protection by design and by default, so the protective setting should be the preselected one.

Best for
Privacy and sharing settings
Use when
Collecting personal data
Avoid when
Sharing is the obvious purpose of the product
Renewals 07 / 10

Auto-Renew Is a Default

Automatic renewal is a default that keeps charging. Show the renewal date, remind people before they are charged, and make turning it off as easy as turning it on.

Best for
Subscriptions and billing
Use when
Plans renew automatically
Avoid when
The renewal would surprise people

Ethics 08 / 10

Defaults Are Decisions

Because most people accept them, defaults are decisions made on users’ behalf. There is no neutral option, so pick the one people would choose with the time and information to decide.

Best for
Design reviews and audits
Use when
Setting any default
Avoid when
Never: every default is a choice

✦

The Default Effect in the Age of AI

AI can predict a different default for every person. That makes defaults more helpful, and more powerful, than ever.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

AI-Chosen Defaults

Models can predict a personal default for each person: the suggested reply, the setting, the plan. Show that it was chosen for them, explain why, and keep the alternatives one tap away.

Shift
One default → predicted defaults
Use when
AI preselects options
Watch for
Defaults nobody can see or explain
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Training Data Is a Default

Whether customer data is used to train AI models is itself a default, and most people never change it. Put the setting in view at the moment data is collected.

Shift
Buried setting → visible choice
Use when
Using customer data for AI
Watch for
Opting everyone in silently
