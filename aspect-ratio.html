Aspect Ratio

The shape of the frame shapes the content.

An image’s width compared with its height, written width:height. The ratio decides what fits in the frame, how content adapts from wide monitors to upright phones, and whether the page jumps while it loads.

Origin

In 1892 William Dickson and Thomas Edison set the frame of 35 mm film at four perforations high, which gave a picture of about 4:3. The sound-era Academy ratio of 1.375:1 stayed close to it, and television adopted 4:3 so that films could be shown on it.

When television drew audiences away in the early 1950s, Hollywood went wide with CinemaScope, VistaVision, and the flat 1.85:1 frame that debuted in 1953, and anamorphic film settled at 2.39:1 in 1970. HDTV standardized on 16:9, computer displays moved from 4:3 through 16:10 to 16:9 by around 2010, and phones made square and vertical video everyday formats.

The Ratio

Aspect ratio is the proportion of width to height. Every frame has one, and when the content and the frame disagree, something has to give: the image is cropped, padded with bars, or stretched. Deciding which, ahead of time, is the design work.

Origin
Dickson and Edison, 35 mm film (1892)
Common ratios
1:1, 4:3, 3:2, 16:9, 9:16
In practice
Reserve the space and crop on purpose
When to Use
  • Placing images, video, or embeds whose size isn’t known until they load.
  • Designing cards, thumbnails, or galleries that mix content of different shapes.
  • Producing media for several screens or platforms, from wide monitors to upright phones.
How to Use
  • Set width and height, or the CSS aspect-ratio property, so the browser reserves space before media loads.
  • Pick one ratio per component and crop to it around a focal point instead of mixing shapes.
  • Choose between cropping and bars deliberately, and never stretch photos, faces, or logos.
Layout shift 01 / 10

Reserve the Space

An image without dimensions loads at zero height, then pushes everything down when it arrives. Width and height attributes, or the CSS aspect-ratio property, let the browser hold the space, which keeps Cumulative Layout Shift low.

Best for
Images, video, and embeds
Use when
The media size is known in advance
Avoid when
Content height is truly unknown, as with user text
Cards 02 / 10

One Ratio per Grid

Thumbnails in mixed shapes make a grid ragged and hard to scan. Crop every image in a component to the same ratio with object-fit: cover, and set a focal point so faces and products stay in the frame.

Best for
Product grids, galleries, and feeds
Use when
Sources come in many shapes
Avoid when
The shape carries meaning, as in art catalogs

Conversion 03 / 10

Crop, Box, or Stretch

When content and frame disagree there are three options: crop to fill, add bars (letterboxing or pillarboxing), or stretch. Crop when the edges don’t matter, add bars when nothing may be cut, and avoid stretching, which distorts everything.

Best for
Video players and embeds
Use when
Source and frame ratios differ
Avoid when
Distortion would be noticed, which is almost always
Screens 04 / 10

Wide on Desktop, Tall on Phones

Desktop monitors are mostly 16:9 or 16:10, while an upright phone is closer to 9:19.5. A hero composed for one is wrong for the other, so serve a different crop at each breakpoint.

Best for
Hero images and banners
Use when
The layout spans phones and desktops
Avoid when
The image works at any crop
Video 05 / 10

Vertical Video Is Native

Square video spread with Instagram and Vine, and stories, Shorts, and Reels made 9:16 the standard on phones. Shoot and edit for the frame people actually hold, not the one you are used to.

Best for
Social and mobile video
Use when
Viewers watch on phones
Avoid when
The video is for TVs or presentations

Photography 06 / 10

Know the Camera Ratios

Most cameras shoot 3:2 or 4:3. Cropping those photos into 16:9 heroes or 1:1 thumbnails throws away part of the shot, so plan the crop when choosing the image, not after.

Best for
Choosing and cropping photos
Use when
Photos must fill fixed slots
Avoid when
The slot matches the source
Sizing 07 / 10

Diagonal Isn’t Size

Screens are sold by their diagonal, but at the same diagonal a 16:9 screen has about 11 percent less area than a 4:3 one, and much less height. Height decides how much of a page fits, so compare screens by their dimensions.

Best for
Choosing target screens and devices
Use when
Comparing displays by diagonal
Avoid when
The ratios being compared are the same
Vocabulary 08 / 10

Ratios Carry a Mood

1:1 feels stable, 4:3 practical, 16:9 cinematic, and 2.39:1 epic. The 16:10 of many laptops is 1.6:1, close to the golden ratio’s 1.618. Choose a frame for the feeling as well as the fit.

Best for
Art direction and hero imagery
Use when
Setting the tone of a page
Avoid when
The platform dictates the frame

Aspect Ratio in the Age of AI

AI now generates and crops images at scale. The frame has to be chosen before generation, and automatic crops have to be checked.

✦ AI Era 09 / 10

Pick the Frame First

Image and video generators compose for the ratio you ask for, and cropping later cuts the composition apart. Choose the ratio for where the image will live before you generate it.

Shift
Crop later → compose for the frame
Use when
Generating images or video
Watch for
Generating square and cropping to wide
✦ AI Era 10 / 10

Check the Smart Crop

Automatic cropping uses saliency models to keep the important part of an image. Twitter dropped its saliency crop in 2021 after its own study found the model favored some faces over others.

Shift
Manual crops → automatic crops
Use when
Cropping images at scale
Watch for
Trusting automatic crops of people unreviewed
